# Chunk sizing and queue capacity

The chain terminal seals a **chunk** once its encoded buffer reaches
`ChunkConfig::target_bytes` (default 64 KiB) and hands it to a per-shard
bounded queue (`SinkOptions::queue_capacity`, default 8). Neither knob bounds
insert size — shard workers merge chunks into full batches. What they govern is
**handoff rate** and **how long a chunk waits before a worker sees it**.

This page exists because both defaults look small, and the obvious instinct is
to raise them. The short answer: **the seal size is not the lever it looks
like, and the queue depth is a latency trade whose price depends entirely on
whether your sink is saturated.** Both defaults stay where they are, but for
narrower reasons than this page originally gave.

:::caution[The two knobs are not independent]
`queue_capacity` is denominated in **chunks**, so queued bytes per shard are
`queue_capacity × chunk.target_bytes`. Holding one fixed while sweeping the
other changes the buffer as well, and the buffer is what most of these curves
actually respond to. Any comparison that means to isolate the seal size must
pin `shards × queue_capacity × chunk.target_bytes` — the *iso-buffer*
construction used further down. Arms that do not are labelled as such.
:::

## Why the earlier thread-scaling curves were misleading

[Framework overhead](framework-overhead.mdx) reports a synthetic ceiling that
peaks at 2 pipeline threads, and [Sink saturation](sink-saturation.mdx) peaks
at 4. Neither is a framework scaling limit: both rigs swept `THREADS` while
holding **egress width fixed** — `pipeline_synthetic` hardcoded 2 I/O workers,
`ch_sink_saturation` pinned shards and `io_threads` across its sweep. Each
curve peaked at roughly its own fixed egress width.

The `pipeline_scaling` rig sweeps threads with `SHARDS` and `IO_THREADS` scaled
alongside (`EGRESS=scaled`) and the in-flight budget derived per arm from the
[sizing rule](../DESIGN.md). Under those conditions the framework keeps
scaling, and the chunk target decides how far.

At 32 MiB of queued buffer the curve flattens and then falls back, while
backpressure pause events climb steeply. At 512 MiB the pauses stay at **zero**
across the whole sweep and scaling efficiency at 8 threads is roughly double.

:::warning[These two series differ in two variables, not one]
This sweep paired each chunk target with a different `QUEUE_CAP`, so the arms
differ in seal size *and* in queued bytes. The charts are still valid for what
they varied — total buffer depth — and that is how they are labelled above.
They cannot attribute the effect to the seal size, and the
[iso-buffer arms](#isolating-the-seal-size) below show that when queued bytes
are held constant the seal size accounts for almost none of it on a realistic
pipeline.
:::

That second chart is the load-bearing one, because the sink is a null writer
that completes **instantly**. There is no sink slowness available to signal, so
every pause is the shard queue filling up.

:::info[Correction: this trigger is capacity-driven]
This page previously read the chart above as showing a **rate-driven** trigger —
pauses caused by chunks arriving faster than workers could service them,
independent of depth. That conclusion did not survive an iso-buffer re-run. Give
the 64 KiB arm the *same* 512 MiB of queued bytes the 1 MiB arm had
(`CHUNK_KIB=64 QUEUE_CAP=1024`, in `pipeline-chunk-isobuffer.jsonl`) and the
pause count drops to **zero**. The pauses tracked buffer depth, which the paired
sweep happened to vary alongside the seal size. Arrival rate matters only
through how fast it fills the buffer you gave it.
:::

## The same lever against a real sink

The synthetic rig runs an order of magnitude above any real sink, so the
question is whether the lever survives contact with one. It does, though
substantially attenuated — and it is not free.

**There is no knee.** Throughput and flush latency climb in near lockstep
across the whole range, so no chunk target dominates the others. The shipped
64 KiB default sits deliberately at the low-latency end. Raise it when you have
the specific symptom — backpressure pauses while the sink itself is idle — and
accept the latency in exchange.

:::caution[Pin the budget when comparing chunk sizes]
The [sizing rule](../DESIGN.md) makes the in-flight budget a function of
`chunk.target_bytes`, so a naive A/B that lets the budget float changes **two**
variables at once. Doing that here inflated the apparent gain by roughly a
third. Every arm above pins `MAX_INFLIGHT_MB` to the same value.
:::

## Isolating the seal size

Every arm above varies queued bytes alongside the seal size. These pin
`shards × queue_capacity × chunk.target_bytes` constant, pairing each seal size
with an inversely scaled `QUEUE_CAP`, so **only seal frequency changes**.

<BenchBars
  bench="pipeline_chunk_isobuffer"
  metric="records_per_s"
  category="chunk_kib"
  emphasize={1024}
  labels={chunkLabels}
  title="Synthetic rig, queued bytes pinned at 512 MiB"
  caption="8 threads, 32 lanes, budget pinned at 8 GiB. QUEUE_CAP scales inversely with the seal size (1024 down to 8) so every arm queues 512 MiB. Medians of 3."
/>

On the synthetic rig the seal size still matters enormously: 1 MiB peaks at
100.8M rec/s against 28.2M at 64 KiB and 28.4M at 8 MiB — **3.6× either way**,
with zero backpressure at every point. Both ends lose for different reasons.
Small chunks pay per-seal overhead; large ones stop fitting in cache, and p50
latency more than doubles at 4–8 MiB.

That rig performs **one memcpy per record**. A seal therefore sits against
roughly 3 µs of work at 64 KiB, so its overhead is most of the per-record
budget. That is a framework ceiling, not a working pipeline.

Now the same construction against a real Avro decoder and a real ClickHouse
sink, queued bytes pinned at 64 MiB (`e2e_chunk_isobuffer`, medians of 5):

| Seal size | Paired `QUEUE_CAP` | rows/s | vs 64 KiB | Rep spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 64 KiB | 128 | 27,448,131 | — | 44.9% |
| 256 KiB | 32 | 27,732,201 | +1.0% | 23.4% |
| 1 MiB | 8 | 25,574,781 | −6.8% | 33.1% |
| 4 MiB | 2 | 27,980,749 | +1.9% | 38.8% |

**±7% against 23–45% rep spread: no signal at all.** These are deliberately
tabulated rather than charted — bars would imply a shape that the spread does
not support. Once a pipeline does real per-record work — decode, explode,
encode — a seal sits against roughly 100 µs instead of 3 µs, and its overhead
falls from most of the per-record budget to about 2%.

**So the seal size is not a production lever.** Its apparent effect is queued
bytes wearing a disguise, and the residue that is genuinely seal frequency is
only visible on a rig that does no work. Leave `chunk.target_bytes` at
64 KiB; if you need more buffer, `queue_capacity` buys it without the cache
penalty that 4–8 MiB chunks carry.

roughly 8× flush p99 for 3.7% throughput.

Here the picture inverts. Throughput climbs **+86% by capacity 64** and pause
events reach zero there, while flush p99 does not move at all. The shallow
default was not protecting latency — it was throttling a sink that had room to
spare, holding throughput at 5.9M rows/s where the server could take 11.3M.

The deser-bound arm agrees (+86% at capacity 256, pauses 51 → 8), so this is not
specific to a MergeTree write.

This one is **not** a smooth trade, and the shape matters more than the
endpoints. Moving off the default to the next step up pays roughly an order of
magnitude of flush p99 for a few percent of throughput — a clearly bad trade,
because the queue bounds the latency of whatever sits in it and the first step
away from a shallow queue is where almost all of that latency appears. Past
that point the curve inverts: the deepest arm buys substantially more
throughput for comparatively little further latency.

So the practical guidance is not "keep it small" — it is **"find out which
regime you are in first."** Deeper queues raised throughput in every regime
measured, by 46% to 92%. What changes is the price:

| Your sink | Throughput from depth | Flush p99 cost | Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saturated (it is the constraint) | +46% at capacity 1024 | ~10× | Real trade. If you move, **move decisively** — an intermediate depth pays most of the latency for little of the throughput. |
| Has headroom (something upstream limits you) | +86% by capacity 64 | none measured | Raise it. The shallow default is throttling you and costing nothing to fix. |

**The default stays at 8** because a shallow queue fails in the direction that
is easier to diagnose: it under-uses a fast sink while keeping flush latency low
and a full queue meaningful as a saturation signal. The opposite default hides a
slow sink behind a buffer. But that is a defensive choice, not a free one — if
you are throughput-dominant, the measurements above say you are leaving a lot on
the table.

**Which regime am I in?** Compare pause events against
`spate_sink_write_duration_seconds{outcome="ok"}` — the per-attempt write, not
the `sink_flush_p99` these arms report. Flush duration spans seal to settle, so
it carries the wait for an `inflight.max_per_shard` slot and rises under
queueing whether or not the sink slowed down; that ambiguity sends this exact
diagnosis the wrong way (see [What a flush
contains](../METRICS.md#what-a-flush-contains)). Pauses high *and* write p99
high means the sink is genuinely behind — depth will cost you latency. Pauses
high with write p99 flat and low means the queue is throttling a sink that has
room: raise `queue_capacity` and watch the pauses go to zero.

The arms below were recorded before the split families existed, so they report
flush p99 only — the correct family for *what depth costs*, which is the
question this page asks, and the wrong one for *is my sink slow*.

:::note[Batch fullness is downstream of this]
A queue too shallow to keep the batcher fed can starve it into hitting `linger`
before `batch.max_rows`, producing partial inserts. In the synthetic arms,
batches ran 33–49% full at the settings that throttled and 101% full at the
setting that did not. That matters more against ClickHouse than these rigs can
show — a null writer creates no parts, so the merge pressure that small inserts
cause is invisible here. If you want **larger** inserts the levers are
`batch.max_rows` and `linger`; queue depth only keeps the rate high enough to
reach the target you set.
:::

## Reading the pause counter

`spate_backpressure_pause_events_total` is **not** on its own a misconfiguration
signal. In the queue-capacity arms above, the configuration with the best
latency also recorded the *most* pauses. Pauses mean the source was throttled;
whether that is healthy depends on what was throttling it.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Lever |
|---|---|---|
| Pauses high, sink **write** p99 flat and low | queue too shallow for a sink that has headroom | raise `SinkOptions::queue_capacity` |
| Pauses high, sink **write** p99 high | sink genuinely behind | more shards / `inflight.max_per_shard` / a faster sink |
| Pauses high, neither of the above, budget near the cap | budget undersized | apply the sizing rule |

The first row previously named `chunk.target_bytes` as the lever. That was
wrong: the iso-buffer arms show the seal size is not what those pauses respond
to. Queued bytes is, and `queue_capacity` buys them without the cache penalty
large chunks carry.

:::warning[`spate_backpressure_inflight_bytes` cannot answer the third row today]
The family is registered, but nothing in the runtime ever calls its setter —
`BackpressureMetrics::set_inflight_bytes` has no production caller, so the
series renders `0` forever rather than being absent. That is worse than a
missing metric: a dashboard cannot distinguish it from a genuinely empty budget.
Until it is wired, diagnose the budget from `max_inflight_bytes` against the
[sizing rule](../DESIGN.md) arithmetic rather than from the gauge.
:::

:::note[`spate_queue_full_events_total` is blind in these rigs]
That counter is registered by `ShardQueues::attach_metrics`, which is only
reached through the pipeline **builder**. Every benchmark rig assembles the
runtime directly, so the series is structurally always zero there and cannot be
used as a validity check. The pause counter and flush histogram are the usable
signals. Pipelines built through the normal builder API do record it.
:::

## Caveats

Single 18-core machine; ClickHouse runs in a container on the same cores, so
absolute rates are not a cluster result — the within-run A/B is what these arms
establish. The two queue-capacity regimes above differ in ClickHouse CPU budget
(capped at 8 versus uncapped) and are not comparable on absolute rate; each is
internally consistent, and the contrast between them is the point. `pipeline_scaling` arms stay within
`threads + io_threads + 1 <= cores`; the rig marks any arm that exceeds it
`OVERSUBSCRIBED` in its note, and none of the published arms do. The synthetic
rig's payload is low-entropy filler, which flatters compression-free paths.
Reproduce with the invocations in [Methodology](methodology.mdx).
