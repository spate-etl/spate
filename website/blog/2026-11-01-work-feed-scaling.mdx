When eight hosts read one S3 prefix, Spate 0.3's split coordination kept 67% of
the throughput the same hosts reached on a static split, and on one host it kept
97.6%. Spate 0.4 will replace it with a work feed on SQS or Kafka. At eight
hosts the SQS feed kept 99.7% and the Kafka feed 97.6%, and on small objects the
feeds read at 3.5 times coordination's rate.

<!-- truncate -->

## Why I replaced split coordination with a queue

I replaced split coordination because of what it cost to keep correct. In 0.3, a
leader instance listed the prefix, cut it into splits, and leased each split to
an instance through a shared NATS store. Every assignment, renewal, and
completion was a fenced write to that store, so that two instances could never
commit the same split, and more often than not, that was where the bugs were.
Fourteen known defects were still open in the protocol when I deprecated it in
0.3, and [#1111](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/issues/1111) lists them.

A queue already does that job. It hands each message to one reader at a time and
hides it while it is read, and when the reader dies, it delivers the message
again. Object stores already publish their "object created" notifications to a
queue: S3 publishes them to SQS, and Ceph to Kafka. So in 0.4, a lister run will
publish the prefix's objects to a work feed on SQS or Kafka as units of work,
and every instance will read its units from the feed directly.
[ADR-0080](/docs/adr/a-work-feed-replaces-leased-fenced-split-coordination)
records the decision, and the [work feed](/docs/user-guide/concepts/work-feed)
page describes the model.

The deprecation went out on maintenance grounds, and I wanted to know what the
switch did to throughput before it shipped, so I measured both on AWS.

## How the benchmark ran

Each host was a `c8g.16xlarge` (Graviton4, 64 vCPUs) in one availability zone of
`eu-west-2`, with three `m8g.xlarge` nodes running the NATS cluster and three
running Kafka. Each host read eight replicas of a corpus of 418 NDJSON objects.
Each object was 1 GiB when decoded and was compressed with ZSTD at level 6, so a
host read 3,344 objects and 5.88 billion rows in every arm. The rows were parsed
with `simd-json` and handed to a sink that discarded them once acknowledged.
This meant that the runs measured the read side of a pipeline and left the
warehouse out.

Every arm ran 48 pipeline threads, 12 I/O workers, and 96 units in flight per
host, with `prefetch_bytes: 32MiB` and `chunk_bytes: 2MiB`, and both versions
used the same settings. Throughput is the fleet's decoded bytes per second over
a common window, from 30 seconds after the last host started to the moment no
work was left to start. Coordination and each feed ran three times at every
fleet size of one, two, four, and eight hosts, and each static split ran once.

The ceiling is a static split, where each host reads its own eight replicas with
no coordination at all. I measured it on both versions, because a 0.4 host read
2.4–2.7% faster than a 0.3 host at every fleet size before any coordination was
involved. Each arm below is set against the static split of its own version, so
that difference counts neither for nor against coordination.

On one host, coordination cost 2.4% against its own static split, and the two
feeds cost 0.3% and 0.5%. From two hosts on, the gap between coordination and
the feeds widened as the fleet grew. At eight hosts, coordination kept 67.1%,
and its three runs spread across a range of 5.24 GB/s. Its last row landed
461–506 seconds after the start, against 316 seconds for the static split. The
SQS feed kept 99.5–99.9% at every fleet size with no unit delivered twice, and
its runs fell within 0.09 GB/s of each other. The Kafka feed matched it up to
four hosts and kept 97.6% at eight.

## Where the time went

Coordination's loss has three links, and the runs measured each of them. The
first is the store. NATS never ran short of CPU, and its nodes averaged 10–17%
of one core at eight hosts. Its operations still slowed as the fleet grew. On a
follower host, the 99th percentile of a write rose from about 25 ms at two hosts
to about 100 ms at eight, and the 99th percentile of a delete from about 50 ms
to about 250 ms.

The second is the wait between splits. A host reads one unit or split per lane,
and each lane is pinned to one pipeline thread. A lane on a follower host that
finished a split waited for its next assignment, and that wait grew with the
fleet, from 0.36 seconds per hand-off at two hosts to 1.35–2.40 seconds at
eight. The leader's own host, by comparison, kept 97–99% of its lanes busy from
two hosts to eight.

The third is what the pipeline threads did while lanes waited. In 0.3 they kept
polling the lanes that had nothing to read. At eight hosts, a coordinated host
polled its source 3.8–7.0 million times a second, against 76,000–91,000 on the
0.3 static split. Its pipeline threads kept 42–45 cores busy and decoded 5.4–8.9
GB/s, where the 0.3 static split decoded 11.3 GB/s on 46 cores. On the same
hosts, the 0.4 SQS feed polled about 30,000 times a second.

The runs measure each link but do not separate their shares of the loss. A
slower store lengthens the wait, and a longer wait gives the threads more empty
polls, so the three compound.

## Small objects widen the gap to 3.5 times

A second corpus cuts the same data into 868,608 objects of 16 MiB decoded, each
about 2.1 MB compressed, for 1.84 TB stored, and four hosts read it.
Coordination hit the 15-minute cap on each of its three runs, and read 13.10
GB/s on average over its window. The SQS feed read 45.95 GB/s and the Kafka feed
read 45.53 GB/s, so the feeds ran at 3.5 times coordination's rate. Each feed
read every row once, with no unit delivered twice. Both versions pack small
objects into shared units, so packing does not explain the gap, and I have not
traced what does.

## What a dead host costs on each feed

At four hosts, I killed one host with `SIGKILL` once it had acknowledged half
its share of the rows, and I repeated this three times on each feed. No row was
lost on either feed, and each of the 96 units the dead host held was delivered
again to another host exactly once.

The feeds differ in how they get there. SQS hands a unit back once its
visibility period ends, which is 60 seconds by default on the SQS feed. The 96
units therefore waited out that minute, and the fleet's last row landed at
368–369 seconds, against 315 seconds without a kill. Kafka reassigns the dead
host's partitions at the next rebalance. That rebalance revokes every partition
in the group, so the fleet read 543–677 units again, including the dead host's
96, and the last row landed at 395–430 seconds, against 323 seconds without a
kill. Every run checked the bound that at-least-once delivery allows, and the
extra rows stayed within it.

## The first feed build was slower on small objects

Before any of it shipped, the first build of the feed was slower than
coordination on small objects. I had already made an unset `units_held` hold two
units per pipeline thread, because a lane pinned to a thread leaves that thread
idle while it waits on its next `GET`
([#1477](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/pull/1477),
[ADR-0086](/docs/adr/an-unset-units-held-holds-twice-the-pipeline-thread-count)).
That was not enough on small objects. The lister published one object per unit,
so each lane finished one small object and then waited for its next unit while
the `GET` ran. On one host with 96 units in flight, only 35.4 of 48 pipeline
threads were busy ([#1483](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/issues/1483)).
Coordination had packed small objects into splits of about 16 all along.

I made the lister pack up to 16 small objects into one unit
([#1500](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/pull/1500),
[ADR-0085](/docs/adr/the-lister-packs-small-objects-into-shared-work-units)). On
the same host and corpus, that lifted decoded throughput 1.30 times with 96
units in flight, from 8.46 to 11.02 GB/s, and 46.5 pipeline threads were then
busy. With 48 units in flight it lifted throughput 1.40 times, from 5.38 to 7.52
GB/s.

Packing gave each lane a next object to start while it decoded the current one,
which led to the second fix. A lane reads each object in windows of
`prefetch_bytes`, and in 0.4 it will read its next window while it hands on the
current one ([#877](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/issues/877),
[#1505](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/pull/1505),
[ADR-0087](/docs/adr/a-lane-reads-its-next-window-while-it-hands-on-the-current-one)).
That change lifted throughput on small objects 1.31 times with one unit per
pipeline thread.

## What running a feed job asks of you

Running a job on the feed asks more of you than coordination did.

- You provision the queue or the topics. An [SQS
  job](/docs/user-guide/connectors/feeds/sqs) needs a standard queue with a
  dead-letter queue that keeps its messages longer than the work queue does, and
  a [Kafka job](/docs/user-guide/connectors/feeds/kafka) needs four topics:
  work, retry, dead-letter, and attempt. Spate checks them at startup and
  refuses a setup it cannot run on, and it creates none of them.
- A bounded job's listing is a run of its own. The lister publishes every unit
  and writes an end marker, and the workers finish only once they have read it.
- A bounded job ends only once its feed has read empty for a settling window,
  five minutes unless you set `settling_window`. The SQS feed refuses a window
  shorter than a minute, because AWS documents a queue's message counts as
  taking that long to settle.
- If the store never delivers a notification, Spate does not list the prefix
  again to recover it
  ([ADR-0072](/docs/adr/missed-storage-notifications-are-not-reconciled-by-re-listing)).
- The Kafka feed runs eager assignment, because its offset tracker relies on a
  rebalance revoking every partition, and that is why a dead host costs Kafka
  more than SQS.

## What the numbers do not show

The runs used one host type, two cuts of one corpus, and one decoder. The sink
discarded every row, so a warehouse that cannot take 90 GB/s would bound every
arm long before coordination did. Kafka's 97.6% at eight hosts came from four or
five hosts in each run keeping 95.0–97.6% of their lanes busy, while the rest
kept 98.3–100%. I have not traced why those hosts fell behind, or the cause of
the small-object gap.
