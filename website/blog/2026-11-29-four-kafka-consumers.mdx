In a Kafka to ClickHouse benchmark, two librdkafka consumers in one Spate
process read 22% faster than one, and four read only 11% faster. The four
consumers were waiting out librdkafka's `fetch.queue.backoff.ms`. It stops
fetching a full partition queue for a second, and draining the queue does not
end the wait. With the backoff at 10 ms, four consumers ran 17% faster than at
the 1000 ms default, so Spate's Kafka source now sets 10 ms itself.

<!-- truncate -->

## One consumer fetched 32 partitions one request at a time

The rig ran the [benchmark](https://spate.kainth.dev/benchmarks)'s Kafka to
Avro to ClickHouse pipeline on one `c8gd.metal-24xl`, a Graviton4 host. A single
Redpanda v26.1.13 broker ran on 3 cores and held a 32-partition topic of 40M
Confluent-framed Avro messages. Each message is about 4 KB and carries a batch
of sensor events, and the pipeline flattened the topic into 2.94B rows in total.
ClickHouse 26.8 ran on 48 cores of the same host. Spate ran in a 32-CPU quota
with 32 pipeline threads and `io_threads: 2`, and inserted in ClickHouse's
Native format with `compression: lz4`.

Every figure in this post comes from tuning runs on November 2, 2026. Each
configuration ran twice, interleaved with an A/A control run. All of the
configurations are on the benchmark repository's
[`bootstrap/spate-1651-consumers`](https://github.com/spate-etl/benchmark/tree/bootstrap/spate-1651-consumers)
branch. None of them are on the benchmark page. Spate was pinned to an earlier
head of [#1653](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/pull/1653), the pull request
that added consumers, and the commits that followed that head did not change
the per-record path.

With one consumer, Spate drained the topic at 34.9M rows/s, averaged over four
drains. The pipeline threads spent 31–32% of their time in the source's poll,
while the consumer's fetch queue held fewer than 200 messages and ClickHouse
delayed under 4% of inserts. The source was waiting on Kafka while the sink had
room to spare ([#1651](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/issues/1651)).

The reason is in how librdkafka fetches. Spate's Kafka source ran one librdkafka
consumer per process and split each partition into a queue of its own that any
pipeline thread could drain. librdkafka keeps at most one Fetch request in
flight per broker connection
([FAQ](https://github.com/confluentinc/librdkafka/wiki/FAQ)), so that one
consumer fetched all 32 partitions from the one broker a request at a time. This
meant that throughput was bounded by the bytes in each Fetch response, times the
requests in flight, divided by a round trip of 8 ms.

## Bigger fetches cost 22% of throughput

The first thing I tried was making each Fetch response carry more bytes. I
raised `fetch.max.bytes` to 100 MiB, `fetch.message.max.bytes` to 8 MiB, and
`queued.min.messages` to 1,000,000 on the one consumer. Over two drains it ran
at 27.3M rows/s, 21.6% slower than the defaults. Each round trip rose from 8 ms
to 32–33 ms, and the pipeline threads' time in the poll rose from 31% to 50–52%.
This run kept librdkafka's default refill backoff, `fetch.queue.backoff.ms` at
1000 ms.

## Two consumers moved the bound to ClickHouse

Spate 0.5 adds a `consumers` key to the
[Kafka source](/docs/user-guide/connectors/sources/kafka). Each consumer is a
separate member of the consumer group, with its own share of the partitions and
its own connection to each broker, so each one adds a Fetch in flight per leader
broker. Its partitions become lanes that any pipeline thread drains, just as a
single consumer's partitions do. The reasons for running several consumers in
one process are in
[ADR-0090](/docs/adr/a-kafka-source-runs-a-configured-number-of-consumers).

```yaml
source:
  kafka:
    brokers: localhost:9092
    topic: sensor-events
    group_id: sensor-etl
    consumers: 2
```

On a four-partition topic, each consumer logs the share it accepted:

```text
2026-11-14T21:53:59.587296Z  INFO spate_kafka::source: accepted assignment consumer=0 partitions=2 started_at_end=2 topic=sensor-events
2026-11-14T21:53:59.587415Z  INFO spate_kafka::source: accepted assignment consumer=1 partitions=2 started_at_end=2 topic=sensor-events
```

On the rig, two consumers ran at 42.5M rows/s over two drains, 22.0% faster than
one. The poll share fell to 8–9%, while ClickHouse delayed 54–67% of inserts
because the table had too many active parts. The benchmark harness marked both
drains as bound by the infrastructure, which meant two consumers had moved the
bound from the source to ClickHouse.

## Four consumers waited on empty partition queues

With ClickHouse as the bound, I expected four consumers to run at about the
speed of two. They ran at 38.6M rows/s, only 10.8% faster than one, while
ClickHouse delayed fewer inserts, 22–36%, and the poll share climbed back to
22–23%. Each consumer's fetch queue held 40,000–58,000 messages, so the data had
reached the process, yet the pipeline threads were still waiting for it
([#1659](https://github.com/spate-etl/spate/issues/1659)).

The harness marked the four-consumer drains as bound by the infrastructure too,
so I swapped the target table for a copy on ClickHouse's `Null` engine, which
parses each insert and stores nothing. One consumer ran at 37.0M rows/s, two at
46.9M, four at 39.8M, and eight at 38.7M. Four consumers still trailed two, with
ClickHouse on about 9 of its 48 cores and Redpanda on at most 1.4 of its 3, so
nothing outside Spate was near its limit.

A third run recorded the depth of every partition queue, and took a 15-second
off-CPU profile of the pipeline threads in one four-consumer drain. The threads
were off the CPU for 23% of their time, and 98% of that was in librdkafka's
`rd_kafka_q_pop`, waiting for a message to arrive, and none of it was spent on
a mutex. In both four-consumer drains of that run, 17 of the 32 partition queues
reached 90% of their byte cap, and 24–55% of the queue samples were empty, so
the queues were swinging between full and empty.

That pattern is librdkafka's refill backoff. When a partition's queue reaches
`queued.min.messages` or `queued.max.messages.kbytes`, librdkafka stops fetching
that partition for `fetch.queue.backoff.ms`, which defaults to 1000 ms, and
draining the queue does not end the wait early
([`rdkafka_fetcher.c`](https://github.com/confluentinc/librdkafka/blob/v2.12.1/src/rdkafka_fetcher.c#L1362-L1381)).
Spate splits a queue per partition, so the byte cap of 65,536 KB, about 16,000
of these messages, applies to each partition on its own. A pipeline thread
usually drains one partition, and it empties a full queue well inside a second,
leaving it to poll an empty queue for the rest of the backoff. One consumer
never filled its queues, so the 1000 ms default had no effect until more
consumers filled them.

## Why the source sets `fetch.queue.backoff.ms` to 10 ms

I made 10 ms the Kafka source's default for `fetch.queue.backoff.ms` because
raising `consumers`, the step the tuning page tells you to take, is what fills
the partition queues and starts the refill stall. Finding the cause took me a
per-partition queue record and an off-CPU profile, and a note on the tuning page
alone would leave you to repeat that work. The default gives way to the source's `rdkafka` map, so a
`fetch.queue.backoff.ms` set there still wins. The
[refill backoff](/docs/user-guide/connectors/sources/kafka/performance-tuning#refill-backoff)
section of the tuning page explains the setting and the 10 ms value I measured.

librdkafka's own documentation of the property points the same way. It says to
decrease it when the
queue thresholds are low and the application sees delays of about a second
between messages, and warns that low values may increase CPU use
([CONFIGURATION.md](https://github.com/confluentinc/librdkafka/blob/v2.12.1/CONFIGURATION.md)).

## What a 10 ms refill backoff bought on the same rig

The last run set `fetch.queue.backoff.ms` through the `rdkafka` map, against the
`Null` table, with two drains per configuration. Four consumers went from 38.4M
rows/s at 1000 ms to 44.8M at 10 ms, 16.7% faster. Each poll took 0.33 ms where
it had taken about 4 ms, and returned 508–510 messages against the source's cap
of 512 per poll.

Two consumers ran at 48.2M rows/s at 10 ms, and eight at 48.5M. With the shorter
backoff, two, four, and eight consumers all used the whole 32-CPU quota and were
throttled, so on this rig the bound past two consumers is CPU.

## What the shorter backoff and more consumers cost

At one consumer, where the queues never fill, the 10 ms refill backoff changed
neither throughput nor CPU use. Both settings ran at 37.2M rows/s, on 22.8–23.5
cores at 1000 ms and 22.9–23.0 at 10 ms. I have not measured a case where the
low value raises CPU use, as librdkafka warns it may.

Each consumer has a fixed cost. It runs a set of librdkafka threads, holds
broker connections and up to one `fetch.max.bytes` response in flight per leader
broker, and joins the group as a separate member. The group has
`replicas × consumers` members, and because the source uses eager assignment,
every rebalance revokes the partitions of all of them.

Four consumers still ran 7% below two at the quota, 44.8M rows/s against 48.2M,
and I have not found out why. The rig cannot show what a third or fourth
consumer buys either, because two already reach the CPU quota.

If your Kafka source spends a large share of its time in the poll,
[More consumers in one process](/docs/user-guide/connectors/sources/kafka/performance-tuning#more-consumers-in-one-process)
shows how to confirm that the fetch path is the bound, and how far to raise the
count.
